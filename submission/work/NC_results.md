### The risk set and the dating problem

The released compilation contains 1,029 provincial-level new bird records with a parseable discovery date for 1,028 (99.9%). Of these, 684 could be matched to a species × province cell in the candidate pool defined by species-distribution models plus forced inclusion of every observed event; 657 fall within the 2002–2024 analysis window (Supplementary Table S7). Twenty-six records discovered before 2002 describe species already present at the start of the window and were removed from the risk set as prevalent rather than incident cases.

Publication lags discovery by a median of one year and a mean of 2.09 years; 81.7% of records lag by at least one year and 26.8% by three or more (Fig. 1a). Re-dating changes the assigned year of 526 of 633 comparable events (83.1%), with a median shift of −1 year and a mean of −1.83 years. The two conventions produce qualitatively different time series (Fig. 1b): the publication-year series rises monotonically to 67 events in 2024, whereas the discovery-year series peaks at 59 in 2019 and falls to 14 in 2024. Neither series is clean: the publication-year rise aggregates delayed reports, and the discovery-year fall is right-censoring, which we corrected directly.

Because the observed hazard under incomplete reporting is approximately the product of the true hazard and the reporting completeness *c*(*t*), and the complementary log-log link is logarithmic in the hazard, *c*(*t*) enters the linear predictor exactly as an offset log *c*(*t*). Completeness was computed for each discovery year from the pooled empirical lag distribution, whose stationarity over the study period we verified (Supplementary Note S1), and the offset is included throughout.

The final risk set at the 50-cell species-distribution-model threshold comprises 8,319 species × province pairs, 185,478 species-province-years and 657 events. After requiring non-missing effort and climate components, the modelling data contain 175,901 rows, 649 events, 392 species and 31 provincial units.

### Publication-year dating inflates the apparent role of survey effort

We decomposed the difference between the previously published specification and the corrected one into five steps, changing exactly one thing at each (Fig. 1c; Table 1).

Re-dating alone moves the survey-effort hazard ratio from 1.788 to 1.305, a reduction of 37% in the excess hazard, and moves the annual-variability hazard ratio from 0.850 (*P* = 1.8 × 10⁻⁴) to 0.971 (*P* = 0.48). The accumulated-warming coefficient is essentially untouched (1.394 → 1.437), as is the interaction (0.876 → 0.859). The remaining corrections are comparatively minor: treating structural zeros in the effort database as coverage gaps moves effort by −0.038; area-weighting the provincial climate series moves it by +0.022; the completeness offset restores +0.159, recovering signal that right-censoring had attenuated; and the province-by-year random intercept removes a further 0.044.

The mechanism is transparent. Publication year and the size of the observational database both increase through time, so assigning an event to its publication year places it in a cell whose recorded effort is systematically higher than the effort that actually generated the observation. It also decorrelates the event from the weather of the year in which the bird was actually seen, which is why a spurious negative coefficient on annual climate variability appears under publication-year dating and disappears under discovery-year dating. Accumulated warming, being a fifteen-year trailing mean, is robust to a shift of one or two years — which is exactly why it survives the correction unchanged.

### Warming and effort contribute comparably

In the frozen main model, both drivers raise the hazard and the effect sizes are similar (Fig. 2a; Table 2). One standard deviation of accumulated warming relative to a species' historical range multiplies the annual hazard by 1.362 (95% CI 1.216–1.524; *P* = 8.3 × 10⁻⁸); one standard deviation of survey effort multiplies it by 1.404 (1.245–1.583; *P* = 2.9 × 10⁻⁸). Annual climate variability has no effect (0.995, 0.909–1.090; *P* = 0.92). The two main effects interact negatively (0.849, 0.761–0.948; *P* = 3.7 × 10⁻³): the marginal effect of warming is steepest where effort is low and flattens where coverage is already dense (Fig. 2b).

Two qualifications follow from the resampling analyses (Supplementary Fig. S11). First, the reference point for the warming coefficient is not unity. Permuting accumulated warming among species within a province-year, or permuting the event indicator among the species at risk in a province-year, still returns a hazard ratio of 1.157–1.167, because both permutations preserve the covariance between provincial-annual climate and provincial-annual event counts. The observed 1.362 lies outside that null distribution (*P* = 0.005 in both cases), but the increment attributable to the species-specific referencing is 1.362/1.157 ≈ 1.18 rather than 1.36; the remainder is a structural baseline shared with the null. The effort coefficient behaves differently: permuting effort across years within a province collapses it to 0.980, with the null 95% interval covering unity, so it derives from the temporal alignment of effort and events rather than from any cross-sectional contrast between well- and poorly surveyed provinces. Second, the Wald intervals are slightly narrow with respect to provincial clustering. A cluster bootstrap resampling provinces widens them to 1.20–1.72 for warming and 1.21–1.76 for effort; both lower bounds still exceed unity, and the interaction remains bounded away from it (0.74–0.99).

In natural units (Fig. 3c), one standard deviation of accumulated warming is **0.179 °C**: a province that has warmed by that much more than a species' own historical Chinese range carries a 36% higher annual hazard for that species. Across the observed range of the variable, −0.65 °C to +0.61 °C, the implied hazard spans roughly ninefold. One standard deviation of survey effort corresponds to a **7.75-fold** increase in annual provincial visits, from a median of 60 visits per year to about 465.

The climate variable is genuinely species-specific, not a relabelled regional index (Fig. 3a, b). Decomposing its variance, 39% of the variation in accumulated warming and 55% of the variation in the raw gradient arise *within* province-years, that is, purely from differences among species. In Yunnan in 2020, accumulated warming ranges from +0.309 °C for *Aethopyga christinae*, whose Chinese range has warmed less than Yunnan has, to −0.280 °C for *Coracias garrulus*, whose range has warmed more. A province-level warming index cannot make that distinction.

Four independent importance criteria agree that warming and effort are of comparable weight, with warming slightly ahead on discrimination (Fig. 2c). Dropping accumulated warming costs 28.7 AIC units and 0.053 of marginal AUC; dropping survey effort costs 29.5 AIC units and 0.032 of marginal AUC. The interaction costs 6.5 AIC units. Annual variability costs −2.0 AIC units, that is, the model is marginally better without it; we retain it because it is one half of a pre-specified decomposition and its null result is itself informative.

The discrimination ladder makes the point most directly (Fig. 2d). With the random structure held at the main model, fixed-effect-only AUC rises from 0.411 for the null model to 0.559 with effort alone, 0.580 with accumulated warming alone, and 0.610 with both. Climate alone discriminates better than effort alone; neither alone approaches the pair.

### Separating the observation process requires a province-by-year level

We evaluated six random-effect structures against four criteria rather than AIC alone (Table 3; Supplementary Fig. S1). Adding a province-by-year intercept to the conventional species + province structure improves AIC by 93.4 units, raises conditional *R*² from 0.274 to 0.411, raises conditional AUC from 0.721 to 0.854, and raises the intraclass correlation from 0.172 to 0.358. Crucially, marginal AUC — the discrimination attributable to fixed effects — is flat across all six structures (0.607–0.613), confirming that the random structure absorbs observational heterogeneity without redistributing the fixed-effect signal.

Two structures with random slopes fit marginally better — a species-specific slope on accumulated warming (ΔAIC 4.5) and a province-specific slope on effort (ΔAIC 0.0, the AIC optimum) — but both *reduce* conditional *R*² (0.403 and 0.395 respectively, against 0.411), and their fixed effects are indistinguishable from those of the main model (effort 1.397 and 1.372; warming 1.348 and 1.366; Supplementary Fig. S1). We therefore freeze the three-level intercept structure as the main model: it is the most parsimonious of the three indistinguishable options, it has the highest explanatory power, and every term has a direct ecological or observational reading. No variance component collapses in any structure (minimum standard deviation 0.274).

### What each level of the model means

Each random term corresponds to a distinct process, and the fitted magnitudes are themselves results (Fig. 3d, Fig. 4; Supplementary Table S2).

*Species (SD 0.405).* Species differ in intrinsic detectability for reasons no covariate in the model captures: body size, song conspicuousness, habitat accessibility, population density and taxonomic attention. One standard deviation multiplies the hazard by 1.50, and the fitted species span a 3.7-fold range.

*Province (SD 0.326).* Provinces differ in their baseline setting for discovery — area, terrain complexity, habitat diversity, observer population and regional research tradition. One standard deviation multiplies the hazard by 1.39.

*Province by year (SD 0.804).* This is the single largest variance component in the model, and it is the reason the level was added. One standard deviation multiplies the hazard by 2.23 — more than either fixed effect — and the fitted levels span a thirteenfold range (Fig. 4c). Ecologically it represents the scale at which the observation process actually operates: a regional survey campaign, a provincial birding festival, a newly established protected area, or a new local reporting channel raises the documentation rate for one province in one year.

*Reporting completeness offset.* Not a free parameter but a known correction, so it consumes no degrees of freedom. Omitting it attenuates the effort coefficient from 1.372 to 1.245, because censoring is concentrated in the recent, high-effort years.

### Migratory strategy shifts the baseline, not the drivers

Migratory strategy shifts the baseline hazard: adding it as a main effect improves fit (ΔAIC 6.2 relative to the model without it) and is the best model in the ladder. It does not, however, moderate either driver. Adding warming × strategy, effort × strategy, both, or the full three-way warming × effort × strategy each *worsens* AIC, and none is supported by a likelihood-ratio test (*P* = 0.693, 0.854, 0.899 and 0.256 respectively). Fitting each group separately gives the same picture, with almost completely overlapping intervals. Group sample sizes, group-specific hazard ratios, the exclusion of species whose strategy is unrecorded, and a warming × effort interaction confined to long-distance migrants — which we report as a hypothesis for a larger compilation rather than as a result, because the formal test for heterogeneity among groups is not significant — are given in Supplementary Note S3 (Supplementary Fig. S3; Supplementary Table S3).

### Which species, and which provinces

Two complementary questions operate at coarser levels: which species yield a new provincial record at all, and which provinces accumulate more of them. Both were addressed with the trait and count models detailed in Supplementary Note S4 (Supplementary Figs S4, S5; Supplementary Tables S4–S6); we report here the two results that bear on the hazard model.

*Species level.* Across the 1,298 modelled species and three treatments of phylogenetic non-independence, two effects hold. Partial migrants are about twice as likely as residents to yield a new provincial record (odds ratio 1.91–2.50 across treatments, all *P* < 0.001), and range size raises the odds by 1.23–1.46 per standard deviation. This converges with the hazard model from a different direction: partial migrants also carry the highest raw event rate there (0.417% against 0.29–0.33%). Range size must be measured as an **area**, not as a count of provinces occupied, because the trait database was published in 2022 and its provincial counts therefore already contain the records used as the response; the two independent area measures agree, giving 1.456 for global range area and 1.653 for Chinese range area (Supplementary Table S4).

*Province level.* Counts of new records per province are overdispersed relative to Poisson and are modelled as negative binomial. The informative specification treats recent survey effort as an offset, so that the response becomes the discovery rate per unit of effort: provinces already well covered in 2002–2008 then yield fewer records per unit of recent effort (0.71, *P* = 0.028), as does higher GDP per capita (0.61, *P* = 0.0027). Hierarchical partitioning attributes 51.2% of the explained deviance to species richness, 33.3% to area and 9% to the two effort terms combined, with the full model explaining 20.5% of deviance.

Effort therefore appears at the provincial scale not as a count effect but as diminishing returns — the same signal the hazard model shows as an effort coefficient that falls through time. Two analyses at different levels converge: China's observational capacity is now large enough that additional effort recovers progressively less of what remains undocumented.

### New records are strongly biased toward the east and northeast

Were new records simply filling survey gaps at random, they would fall on all sides of a species' known range with equal frequency. They do not. Taking the bearing from the centroid of each species' BirdLife range to the record itself and binning into eight sectors, direction is far from uniform (χ² = 580.1, df = 7, *P* = 4.6 × 10⁻¹²¹; Fig. 5). Two sectors are over-represented after Holm correction: east holds 237 of 642 records (36.9%, against 80.2 expected) and northeast 179 (27.9%). South and west are nearly empty, with 20 and 19.

The pattern is not carried by one taxon: five of the well-sampled orders reject uniformity on their own — Passeriformes (χ² = 376.6, *P* = 2.5 × 10⁻⁷⁷), Anseriformes (64.0), Accipitriformes (52.5), Charadriiformes (45.5) and Columbiformes (29.1) — and in every case the significant sectors are east, northeast or both.

Two mechanisms are consistent with this and cannot be separated by these data. The eastern half of China holds the great majority of the observer population, so a species' range edge is far more likely to be watched on its eastern than on its western side; this is the observational reading, and it is the same asymmetry the province-by-year variance component captures. The ecological reading is that eastern and north-eastern range margins are where suitable climate is expanding fastest for a warming-tracking assemblage. That birds show an eastward rather than a northward bias tilts the balance toward the observational reading: China's observer gradient runs west-to-east, not south-to-north.

### The return on survey effort is declining

The proportional-hazards assumption holds for accumulated warming (interaction with time β = −0.022, *P* = 0.82) but not for effort (β = −0.211, *P* = 7.7 × 10⁻⁴). This is a substantive result rather than a diagnostic failure. Allowing the effort coefficient to vary through time, its hazard ratio falls from 2.18 (95% CI 1.63–2.90) in 2002 to 1.07 (0.88–1.30) in 2024, crossing into non-significance in the early 2020s (Fig. 4d). Two decades of intensifying observation have used up the readily detectable gaps, so an additional standard deviation of effort now yields far fewer first records than it did at the start of the period. That the climate term shows no such decline is the sharpest available evidence that the two coefficients are capturing different processes: a saturating observational process and a non-saturating climatic one.

### Robustness

The conclusions do not depend on any freely chosen analytical decision (Fig. 6; Supplementary Note S5).

*Species-distribution-model threshold.* Raising the requirement from 50 to 200 suitable cells changes the risk set from 175,901 to 155,435 rows but leaves the coefficients essentially fixed (effort 1.404, 1.405, 1.411; warming 1.362, 1.376, 1.372; interaction 0.849, 0.851, 0.850).

*Accumulation window and climate baseline.* We tested windows of 5, 10, 15 and 20 years jointly against three series (WorldClim 1980–2000, CRU 1980–2000 and CRU 1970–2000; the 1970–2000 baseline cannot be computed from the WorldClim downscaling) (Supplementary Fig. S2). The warming coefficient rises with window length in every series, from about 1.16–1.21 at five years to 1.36–1.40 at twenty years. This is the signature expected of a process that integrates climate over years to decades: a five-year window measures weather rather than climate and dilutes the signal, whereas a boundary shifts only when conditions have been favourable long enough for colonisation and establishment. A finer grid spanning 3 to 23 years (Supplementary Table S8) places the optimum at 18–20 years with AIC rising again by 23 years, so the optimum is interior rather than an artefact of the tested range. We retain 15 years as pre-specified; it lies within 2.2–5.6 AIC units of the optimum in every series. The choice of baseline period, of data source and of climate indicator moves the warming estimate only slightly (Supplementary Note S5).

Across all twelve combinations the effort coefficient is flat at 1.34–1.41, and the interaction is negative throughout (0.82–1.00). If the climate and effort terms were competing for the same variance, changing the climate specification would move the effort estimate. It does not, which is direct evidence that the two are separately identified.

*Effort proxy and missing-data rule.* All twelve combinations of four proxies (visits, observers, birding days, records) and three treatments of the 24 implausible zero cells (coverage gap, bounded within-province interpolation, zero-filling) give effort hazard ratios between 1.25 and 1.52 and warming hazard ratios between 1.35 and 1.37. The evidence that these cells are gaps in database coverage rather than years in which birdwatching ceased is given in Supplementary Note S5.

### Model adequacy

Simulation-based residual diagnostics indicate a well-calibrated model (Supplementary Fig. S9; Supplementary Table S1). Scaled residuals are uniform (Kolmogorov–Smirnov *P* = 0.74), dispersion is correct (*P* = 0.59) and there is no zero inflation (*P* = 0.59). Province-mean residuals show no spatial autocorrelation (Moran's *I* = −0.038; permutation *P* = 0.86). The outlier test is significant (95 outliers), as is expected of a binary outcome with an event rate of 0.37%.

Three resampling analyses complete the assessment (Supplementary Note S6; Supplementary Fig. S11). A cluster bootstrap shows that provincial clustering inflates the standard errors by a factor of 1.26–1.59 relative to the model-based values, whereas species clustering does not (0.86–1.17); Table 2 reports both. A parametric bootstrap recovers the coefficients with absolute bias below 0.01 on the log-hazard scale and gives actual coverage of 91.3–95.3% for the nominal 95% interval. Leaving out each province in turn, and each of ten species deciles, moves the coefficients by at most 4.2%, with 9 of 164 combinations exceeding 3%: no single province or species group carries the result.

### How far can these relationships be projected?

We projected the fitted hazard under CMIP6 SSP2-4.5 and SSP5-8.5 for 2030, 2050 and 2080, then asked how much of each scenario lies inside the range over which the model was fitted (Fig. 7b). The answer sharply constrains what can be claimed. The historical species-specific gradient has a standard deviation of only 0.18 °C, because provincial and range-wide anomalies largely move together. Future province-minus-range warming differentials reach 2 °C, more than ten standard deviations beyond the fitted range. Consequently, only 47.8% of species-province cells fall inside the fitted covariate support under SSP2-4.5 in 2030, 10.4% in 2050 and 2.1% in 2080; under SSP5-8.5 the corresponding figures are 34.2%, 2.1% and 1.9%.

Restricted to cells inside the support, the mechanistic model projects hazard ratios relative to 2024 of 0.79 to 2.94 (Supplementary Fig. S6). Applied without the mask, the same model produces ratios up to 78 (Supplementary Fig. S10). We regard the masked surfaces as the defensible result.

A gradient-boosted comparison model trained on the same three predictors reinforces the point (Supplementary Note S7; Supplementary Figs S7, S8). Within the observed range the two model families track each other across province-years (Spearman ρ = 0.54); outside it, rank correlations across province-scenario cells range from −0.04 to 0.18, and the two agree on the direction of change in only 23.7% of cells, falling to 6.5% under SSP5-8.5 in 2080. The mechanistic model projects increases in 176 of 186 cells; the machine-learning model in 42. The cause is structural: tree ensembles return boundary leaf values once covariates leave the training support and therefore saturate, whereas the complementary log-log model extrapolates linearly on the log-hazard scale.

### Reserve records measure the observation process, not reserve performance

Twenty-six per cent of new records fall inside one of the 1,028 mapped Chinese nature reserves, against a land-area share of 10.4% — an apparent 2.5-fold enrichment ordinarily read as evidence that the reserve network is receiving redistributing species. We tested that reading against the observation process, using GBIF/eBird checklists to define availability as *where birdwatchers actually went* and matching each event to controls from the same province and year, weighted by the checklists recorded there (matched design, two counter-tests, persistence of first records and the reserve–warming comparison in Supplementary Note S2; Supplementary Figs S12–S14).

Nationally, birding effort is not concentrated in reserves: the checklist-weighted share inside reserves is 10.8%, indistinguishable from the area share. Conditioning on province and year, the enrichment falls from 2.5-fold to an odds ratio of 1.71 (95% CI 1.39–2.11), and to 1.57 (1.26–1.96) after adding elevation, longitude and two bioclimatic covariates.

The residual association is itself largely observational. It is carried by very few sites: the 172 inside-reserve events fall in only 68 reserves (6.6% of the 1,028), the top two account for 27.3%, and a block bootstrap resampling host reserves rather than records widens the interval to 0.89–3.19. It is also confined to the period of rapid growth in birdwatching: the odds ratio is 2.05 (1.61–2.59) for 2013–2023 but 0.97 (0.62–1.50) for 2002–2012, although the mapped reserve network gained no area after 2012.

Because within-site observation bias cannot be conditioned out, we treat the inference as asymmetric and state the rule in advance: a null result would be strong evidence, a positive result weak. The odds ratio of 1.57 is therefore an upper bound on any ecological contribution, and the operational conclusion is that counts of new records inside reserves should not be used as an indicator of reserve performance. All statements apply only to the 1,028 mapped reserves established before 2012; because reserve status has almost no temporal variation within the window, we make no causal claim.
