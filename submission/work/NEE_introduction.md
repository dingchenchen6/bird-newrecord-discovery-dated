Documented species distributions are a joint product of where organisms occur and where people look<sup>1–4</sup>, and the inferential consequences remain incompletely handled in the literature on climate-driven range shifts<sup>5–8</sup>. A first report of a species from an administrative unit is often read as evidence of range expansion, but the same record is equally consistent with a long-standing population that has only now been detected, or with a stable population in a region whose observer base has recently grown<sup>16,19–21</sup>. Which reading is correct determines whether a body of records is evidence of biodiversity redistribution or of the filling of the Wallacean shortfall<sup>1,2</sup>.

China offers unusual leverage on this problem. Over the past two decades the country has warmed rapidly while its birdwatching community grew from a few thousand to hundreds of thousands of participants, and a national compilation of provincial new bird records now carries dates of discovery as well as of publication<sup>9,25,37</sup>. That both signals expanded together is what makes the attribution hard, and what makes a formal risk-set analysis necessary.

Three gaps motivate this study. First, the temporal alignment of events has not been examined. Records carry two dates — when the bird was seen, and when the paper appeared — and almost all analyses use the latter, the field primary sources reliably report. Publication lag is not a small random offset but a right-skewed delay that grew alongside the observational infrastructure, so dating an event to publication places it in a year in which the effort database was systematically larger. Second, the observation process operates at a scale most models do not represent. Survey campaigns, birding festivals, new protected areas and reporting channels act on one province in one year; species and region random intercepts, the conventional choice<sup>55</sup>, cannot absorb this, and it leaks into whichever fixed effect trends with observational capacity. Third, projections of future record generation have been made without checking whether the scenarios lie inside the fitted data.

Here we address all three. We construct a complete risk set — every species × province × year cell in which a new record could have occurred, with absorbing exit after the first record — and model the annual hazard with a complementary log-log link, the discrete-time analogue of a proportional-hazards model<sup>[A1,A2]</sup>. We re-date every event to its year of discovery, correct the resulting right-censoring with a reporting-completeness offset, treat structurally implausible zeros in the effort database as coverage gaps, and add a province-by-year random intercept. We then ask how large the dating bias is, what warming and effort each contribute once it is corrected, and how far the fitted relationship can be projected.
