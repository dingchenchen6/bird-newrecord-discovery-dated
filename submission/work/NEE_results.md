### The risk set and the dating problem

The released compilation contains 1,029 provincial-level new bird records, with a parseable discovery date for 1,028 (99.9%). Of these, 684 matched a species × province cell in the candidate pool defined by species-distribution models plus forced inclusion of every observed event, and 657 fall within the 2002–2024 analysis window. After requiring non-missing effort and climate components, the modelling data contain 175,901 rows, 649 events, 392 species and 31 provincial units (Supplementary Note S1; Supplementary Table S9).

Publication lags discovery by a median of one year and a mean of 2.09 years; 81.7% of records lag by at least one year and 26.8% by three or more (Fig. 2a). Re-dating changes the assigned year of 526 of 633 comparable events (83.1%), with a median shift of −1 year and a mean of −1.83 years. The two conventions produce qualitatively different time series (Fig. 2b): the publication-year series rises monotonically to 67 events in 2024, whereas the discovery-year series peaks at 59 in 2019 and falls to 14 in 2024. Neither is a clean description of reality: the publication-year rise is an artefact of aggregating delayed reports, and the discovery-year fall is right-censoring. Because the observed hazard under incomplete reporting is approximately the product of the true hazard and the reporting completeness *c*(*t*), and the complementary log-log link is logarithmic in the hazard, *c*(*t*) enters the linear predictor exactly as an offset log *c*(*t*), which we include throughout.

### Publication-year dating inflates the apparent role of survey effort

We decomposed the difference between the previously published specification and the corrected one into five steps, changing exactly one thing at each (Fig. 2c; Supplementary Table S1). Re-dating alone moves the survey-effort hazard ratio from 1.788 to 1.305, a reduction of 37% in the excess hazard, and moves the annual-variability hazard ratio from 0.850 (*P* = 1.8 × 10⁻⁴) to 0.971 (*P* = 0.48). The accumulated-warming coefficient is essentially untouched (1.394 → 1.437). The four remaining corrections — coverage-gap effort, area-weighted climate, the completeness offset and the province-by-year intercept — are smaller, although the completeness offset restores +0.159 by recovering signal that right-censoring had attenuated.

The mechanism is transparent. Publication year and the size of the observational database both increase through time, so assigning an event to its publication year places it in a cell whose recorded effort is systematically higher than the effort that actually generated the observation. This inflates the effort coefficient. It also decorrelates the event from the weather of the year in which the bird was actually seen, which is why a spurious negative coefficient on annual climate variability appears under publication-year dating and disappears under discovery-year dating. Accumulated warming, being a fifteen-year trailing mean, is robust to a shift of one or two years — which is exactly why it survives the correction unchanged.

### Warming and effort contribute comparably

In the frozen main model, both drivers raise the hazard and the effect sizes are similar (Fig. 1a; Table 1). One standard deviation of accumulated warming relative to a species' historical range multiplies the annual hazard by 1.362 (95% CI 1.216–1.524; *P* = 8.3 × 10⁻⁸); one standard deviation of survey effort multiplies it by 1.404 (1.245–1.583; *P* = 2.9 × 10⁻⁸). Annual climate variability has no effect (0.995, 0.909–1.090; *P* = 0.92). The two main effects interact negatively (0.849, 0.761–0.948; *P* = 3.7 × 10⁻³): the marginal effect of warming is steepest where effort is low and flattens where coverage is already dense (Fig. 1b).

Two qualifications apply to these effect sizes. First, the reference point for the warming coefficient is not unity. Permuting accumulated warming among species within a province-year, or permuting the event indicator among the species at risk in a province-year, still returns a hazard ratio of 1.157–1.167, because both permutations preserve the covariance between provincial-annual climate and provincial-annual event counts. The observed 1.362 lies outside that null distribution (*P* = 0.005 in both cases), but the increment attributable to the species-specific referencing is 1.362/1.157 ≈ 1.18 rather than 1.36; the remainder is a structural baseline shared with the null. The effort coefficient behaves differently: permuting effort across years within a province collapses it to 0.980, with the null 95% interval covering unity, so it derives from the temporal alignment of effort and events rather than from any cross-sectional contrast between well- and poorly surveyed provinces. Second, the Wald intervals are slightly narrow with respect to provincial clustering. A cluster bootstrap resampling provinces widens them to 1.20–1.72 for warming and 1.21–1.76 for effort; both lower bounds still exceed unity, and the interaction remains bounded away from it (0.74–0.99). Resampling species instead leaves the intervals essentially unchanged.

In natural units, one standard deviation of accumulated warming is 0.179 °C: a province that has warmed by that much more than a species' own historical Chinese range carries a 36% higher annual hazard for that species. One standard deviation of survey effort corresponds to a 7.75-fold increase in annual provincial visits. The climate variable is genuinely species-specific rather than a relabelled regional index, and four independent importance criteria agree that warming and effort are of comparable weight (Supplementary Note S2; Supplementary Fig. S4).

The discrimination ladder makes the point most directly (Fig. 1d). With the random structure held at the main model, fixed-effect-only AUC rises from 0.411 for the null model to 0.559 with effort alone, 0.580 with accumulated warming alone, and 0.610 with both. Climate alone discriminates better than effort alone; neither alone approaches the pair. This is a different conclusion from the one reached under publication-year dating, where effort dominated.

### Separating the observation process requires a province-by-year level

We evaluated six random-effect structures against four criteria rather than AIC alone (Supplementary Table S2; Supplementary Fig. S1). Adding a province-by-year intercept to the conventional species + province structure improves AIC by 93.4 units, raises conditional *R*² from 0.274 to 0.411, raises conditional AUC from 0.721 to 0.854, and raises the intraclass correlation from 0.172 to 0.358. Marginal AUC is flat across all six structures (0.607–0.613), so the random structure absorbs observational heterogeneity without redistributing the fixed-effect signal. Two random-slope alternatives fit marginally better on AIC but reduce conditional *R*² and leave the fixed effects unchanged, so we freeze the three-level intercept structure (Supplementary Note S3).

The random intercepts have standard deviations of 0.405 for species, 0.326 for province and 0.804 for province by year, so one standard deviation multiplies the hazard by 1.50, 1.39 and 2.23 respectively, and the fitted province-year levels span a thirteenfold range (Fig. 3c). The province-by-year term is the single largest variance component, larger than either fixed effect.

Ecologically, that component is the scale at which the observation process actually operates: a regional survey campaign, a provincial birding festival, a newly established protected area or a new local reporting channel raises the documentation rate for one province in one year, and such shocks are not shared nationally (Supplementary Note S3; Supplementary Table S4). The reporting-completeness offset is a known correction rather than a free parameter; omitting it attenuates the effort coefficient from 1.372 to 1.245, because censoring is concentrated in the recent, high-effort years.

### Migratory strategy shifts the baseline, not the drivers

Migratory strategy shifts the baseline hazard, improving fit by ΔAIC 6.2, but moderates neither driver: warming and effort raise the hazard in residents, partial migrants and long-distance migrants alike, and no interaction with strategy is supported (Supplementary Note S4; Supplementary Table S5; Supplementary Fig. S5).

### Which species, and which provinces

Two companion analyses operate at coarser levels (Supplementary Note S5; Supplementary Tables S6–S8; Supplementary Figs S6, S7). Two species-level effects hold under all three treatments of phylogenetic non-independence: partial migrants are about twice as likely as residents to yield a new provincial record, and range size raises the odds — but only when range is measured as an area, because the count of provinces occupied is contaminated by the response.

At the provincial level, the informative model treats recent effort as an offset, making the response the discovery rate per unit of effort; provinces already well covered in 2002–2008 yield fewer records per unit of recent effort (0.71, *P* = 0.028). Effort therefore appears at the provincial scale not as a count effect but as diminishing returns — the same signal the hazard model shows as an effort coefficient that falls through time.

### New records are strongly biased toward the east and northeast

If new records simply filled in survey gaps at random, they would fall on all sides of a species' known range with equal frequency. They do not. Taking the bearing from the centroid of each species' BirdLife range to the record and binning into eight sectors, direction is far from uniform (χ² = 580.1, df = 7, *P* = 4.6 × 10⁻¹²¹; Fig. 4). Two sectors are over-represented after Holm correction: east holds 237 of 642 records (36.9%, against 80.2 expected) and northeast 179 (27.9%). South and west are nearly empty, with 20 and 19. The pattern is not carried by one taxon: five well-sampled orders reject uniformity on their own, and in every case the significant sectors are east, northeast or both (Supplementary Note S6; Supplementary Fig. S16).

Two mechanisms are consistent with this and cannot be separated by these data. Observationally, the eastern half of China holds the great majority of the observer population, so a range edge is far more likely to be watched on its eastern than on its western side. Ecologically, eastern and north-eastern margins are where suitable climate is expanding fastest for a warming-tracking assemblage. That birds show an eastward rather than a northward bias is itself informative: China's observer gradient runs west-to-east, not south-to-north, which tilts the balance toward the observational explanation for this taxon.

### The return on survey effort is declining

The proportional-hazards assumption holds for accumulated warming (interaction with time β = −0.022, *P* = 0.82) but not for effort (β = −0.211, *P* = 7.7 × 10⁻⁴). This is a substantive result rather than a diagnostic failure. Allowing the effort coefficient to vary through time, its hazard ratio falls from 2.18 (95% CI 1.63–2.90) in 2002 to 1.07 (0.88–1.30) in 2024, crossing into non-significance in the early 2020s (Fig. 3d). Two decades of intensifying observation have used up the readily detectable gaps. That the climate term shows no such decline is the sharpest available evidence that the two coefficients capture different processes: a saturating observational one and a non-saturating climatic one.

### Robustness

The conclusions do not depend on any freely chosen analytical decision (Supplementary Note S7; Supplementary Figs S2, S3; Supplementary Table S10). Raising the species-distribution-model threshold from 50 to 200 suitable cells changes the risk set from 175,901 to 155,435 rows but leaves the coefficients essentially fixed (effort 1.404, 1.405, 1.411; warming 1.362, 1.376, 1.372; interaction 0.849, 0.851, 0.850). All twelve combinations of four effort proxies and three treatments of the 24 implausible zero cells give effort hazard ratios between 1.25 and 1.52 and warming hazard ratios between 1.35 and 1.37. Tested jointly against three series (WorldClim 1980–2000, CRU 1980–2000 and CRU 1970–2000; the 1970–2000 baseline cannot be computed from the WorldClim downscaling), the warming coefficient rises with window length in every series, from about 1.16–1.21 at five years to 1.36–1.40 at twenty — the signature expected of a process that integrates climate over years to decades, with an interior optimum. We retain 15 years as pre-specified; it lies within 2.2–5.6 AIC units of the optimum in every series. The effort coefficient is flat at 1.34–1.41 across the same grid: if the two terms were competing for the same variance, changing the climate specification would move the effort estimate, and it does not.

### Model adequacy

Simulation-based residual diagnostics indicate a well-calibrated model with no spatial autocorrelation in province-mean residuals, apart from a significant outlier test (95 outliers, *P* < 0.001) that is expected of a binary outcome with an event rate of 0.37% (Supplementary Note S8; Supplementary Table S3; Supplementary Figs S8, S9). Three design-preserving permutation nulls, each destroying exactly one association while holding the risk set, the per-province-year event counts, the effort and completeness marginals and the random-effect hierarchy fixed, place every reported coefficient outside its null distribution except annual climate variability; a cluster bootstrap of 300 resamples and a parametric bootstrap of 150 simulations agree that provincial, but not species, clustering inflates the model-based standard errors. Leaving out each province in turn, and each of ten species deciles, shows that no single province or species group carries the result.

### How far can these relationships be projected?

We projected the fitted hazard under CMIP6 SSP2-4.5 and SSP5-8.5 for 2030, 2050 and 2080, applying the four-model ensemble delta to both ends of the climate gradient, and asked how much of each scenario lies inside the range over which the model was fitted (Fig. 5b).

The answer sharply constrains what can be claimed. The historical species-specific gradient has a standard deviation of only 0.18 °C, because provincial and range-wide anomalies largely move together. Future province-minus-range warming differentials reach 2 °C, more than ten standard deviations beyond the fitted range. Consequently, only 47.8% of species-province cells fall inside the fitted covariate support under SSP2-4.5 in 2030, 10.4% in 2050 and 2.1% in 2080; under SSP5-8.5 the corresponding figures are 34.2%, 2.1% and 1.9%.

Restricted to cells inside the support, the mechanistic model projects hazard ratios relative to 2024 of 0.79 to 2.94; applied without the mask, the same model produces ratios up to 78. A gradient-boosted comparison model trained on the same three predictors tracks the mechanistic model inside the observed range but diverges completely outside it, agreeing on the direction of change in only 23.7% of cells (Fig. 5c; Supplementary Note S9; Supplementary Figs S10–S12).

### Records inside nature reserves measure the observation process, not reserve performance

Twenty-six per cent of new records fall inside one of the 1,028 mapped Chinese nature reserves, against a land-area share of 10.4% — an apparent 2.5-fold enrichment that would ordinarily be read as evidence that the reserve network is receiving redistributing species. We tested that reading against the observation process directly, using 2.86 million GBIF/eBird checklists to define availability as where birdwatchers actually went and matching each event to 200 checklist-weighted controls from the same province and year. Conditioned in this way, the enrichment falls to an odds ratio of 1.71 (95% CI 1.39–2.11), and to 1.57 (1.26–1.96) after adding elevation, longitude and two bioclimatic covariates. Because within-site observation bias cannot be conditioned out, we treat 1.57 as an upper bound on any ecological contribution, and the operational conclusion is that counts of new records inside reserves should not be used as an indicator of reserve performance (Supplementary Note S10; Supplementary Figs S13–S15).
